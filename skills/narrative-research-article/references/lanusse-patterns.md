# Lanusse Writing Patterns

Structural patterns extracted from "The Future of Energy is Buried Under the Sea" by Ricky Lanusse (The New Climate, Medium, July 2026). ~2,800 words, 12 min read.

Load this reference before writing articles with the `narrative-research-article` skill.

---

## 1. Structure and Architecture

### The Cinematic Cold Open

The article opens NOT with the thesis, NOT with a definition, but inside a **scene**. The reader is dropped into the boots of a Roman legionnaire mid-march. Sensory details (the leather sandal, the iron studs, the clacking) create immediacy before any argument is made.

**Rule:** Start in a specific, vivid moment. No throat-clearing. No "In today's world of..." The reader should feel *placed*, not *informed*, on line one.

**Example:**
> Your left sandal is already cutting into your heel. The leather is new, and the little iron studs on the bottom bite the road and clack with every step, like they're trying to wake the whole empire up.

### The Bridge Paragraph

After the cold open scene, a single paragraph pivots from the metaphor to the actual subject. This is NOT a transition section. It is a single paragraph that explicitly states the connection.

**Rule:** After your cold open scene, write ONE paragraph that says: "This is the paradox/lesson at the heart of [topic]." Then state the thesis. The bridge is the hinge between imagination and argument.

### Section-by-Section Escalation

The article follows a 7-section architecture, each with a distinct function:

| # | Section Function | Content Type |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cold Open | Scene (Roman soldier) |
| 2 | Concrete Example | Baltic Cable: the "what" |
| 3 | Technical Explanation | The Duck Curve: the "why" |
| 4 | Data and Numbers | EU interconnection targets: the "how big" |
| 5 | Geopolitical Layer | NordLink, Nord Stream sabotage: the "who" |
| 6 | Thematic Elevation | Trust as scarcest resource: the "so what" |
| 7 | Circular Close | Returns to Roman roads metaphor |

**Rule:** Each section does ONE job. Do not mix explanation with data with geopolitics in the same section. The reader should always know which *mode* they are in.

### The Section Divider

Sections are separated by `---` (horizontal rules). No numbered headers, no "Section 1: The Problem." The divider is invisible architecture.

**Rule:** Use horizontal rules or whitespace breaks between major sections. Never number sections or use "First... Second... Finally." Let the content carry the structure.

## 2. Voice and Style

### The Second-Person Immersion

Uses "you" and "your" in the cold open to place the reader inside the scene. Then shifts to third person for the analytical sections. The voice is direct, declarative, and unsentimental.

**Voice markers:**
- Short, punchy sentences: "Nobody cares." (3 words, standalone paragraph)
- Colloquial but precise: "the not-so-heroic stuff"
- No hedging: "The math isn't close." Not "the math seems to suggest"
- Period as a weapon: "The cable is the thing." Finality, authority

### The Two-Sentence Rhythm

Most paragraphs follow a **setup-punchline** cadence. The first sentence sets context. The second sentence delivers the insight or turn. Occasionally a one-sentence paragraph appears as a "hammer" for emphasis.

**Example:**
> This is a legion on the move. And Rome didn't build an empire by pausing the march so everyone could get comfy shoes.

**Example (hammer):**
> Nobody cares. (standalone)

### Concrete Before Abstract

Every abstract claim is preceded by a specific, tangible detail. The author never states a principle without first grounding it in something the reader can see, touch, or imagine.

**Abstract to Concrete mappings:**
- "Infrastructure moves civilization forward" becomes "a 250-kilometer submarine power line beneath the waves"
- "Renewable intermittency is a problem" becomes "the duck curve because the net demand chart looks like a duck"
- "Geopolitics complicates cables" becomes "Russia has been known to run military exercises near undersea cables"

## 3. Research and Data Patterns

### The Specificity Stack

Data is always paired with interpretation. Numbers never stand alone. The formula is: **Number + What it means + Why it matters**.

**Example (three layers):**
1. Raw number: "14 gigawatts of interconnection capacity"
2. Comparison: "roughly equivalent to the output of 14 nuclear power plants"
3. Grounding: "That's 14 nuclear plants' worth of electricity moving through cables you can't see, across ocean floors you'll never visit, governed by treaties you probably haven't read"

### Named Sources and Dates

Every data point traces to a named institution and a specific year:
- "California Independent System Operator since 2013"
- "European Commission estimates approximately 58 billion euros by 2030"
- "International Energy Agency estimates 180-200 billion euros annually by 2050"
- "Nord Stream pipelines were sabotaged in 2022"

**Rule:** Never say "studies show" or "experts say." Name the institution, give the date, provide the number. Specificity equals credibility.

### The Cost-Benefit Frame

When presenting data, the author almost always presents both sides of a cost/benefit comparison, then lets the asymmetry speak for itself.

**Example:**
> "58 billion euros to build the cables" vs. "180-200 billion euros annually to NOT build them"

Followed by: "The math isn't close." One sentence. No elaboration needed.

## 4. Narrative Devices

### The Circular Frame

The article begins and ends with the same metaphor (Roman roads/legionnaires). The closing paragraphs explicitly echo the opening imagery, creating a sense of completeness. But the return is not repetition. It is *elevation*. The metaphor has been enriched by everything in between.

**Opening:**
> Everything that does take up space: the gear, the food, the water. That's what wins wars.

**Closing:**
> The Roman soldier didn't win because he carried everything himself. He won because the empire had supply lines. Roads. Cables. Connections.

### The One-Word Echo

Key words and phrases recur across sections like musical motifs: "the thing," "the road," "the cable." These echoes build cumulative emphasis without explicit restatement.

**Recurring motif progression:**
- "Glorious speeches don't fill stomachs. **Swords win battles, not slogans.**"
- "**The cable is the thing.**"
- "**The road is the thing.**"

### The Rhetorical Question (Used Sparingly)

Only one rhetorical question appears: "So why isn't it happening faster?" It appears at the 70% mark as a pivot from exposition to analysis. The restraint makes it effective.

**Rule:** Use rhetorical questions only once per article, and only as a section pivot. Never as an opener or closer.

### The Emotional Micro-Burst

Within analytical sections, brief emotional beats appear. Not sentimentality. A moment of human connection that prevents the piece from feeling like a whitepaper.

**Examples:**
- "It's heavy in the way that makes you think of your 6-year-old back home"
- "a declaration of mutual dependence: we will keep the lights on for you, and you will keep the lights on for us"
- "trust, in the energy world, is the scarcest resource of all"

## 5. Formula Summary

| Element | Pattern | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | Cinematic cold open scene | Drop reader INTO a moment. No throat-clearing. |
| Bridge | Single pivot paragraph | "This is the paradox at the heart of [X]:" then thesis |
| Body sections | 7 sections, each with ONE job | Scene, Example, Technical, Data, Geopolitics, Theme, Close |
| Data | Specificity Stack | Never a bare number. Always interpret. |
| Voice | Two-sentence rhythm + hammer paragraphs | Setup-punchline. Standalone sentences for emphasis. |
| Tone | Direct, unsentimental, no hedging | "The math isn't close" not "the math seems to suggest" |
| Research | Named institutions + dates | Never "studies show." Always name + year + number. |
| Motif | Recurring one-word echoes | "the thing," "the road," "the cable" as musical motifs |
| Closing | Circular frame (echo opening) | Return to opening metaphor, elevated by everything between |
| Dividers | Horizontal rules, no section numbers | Invisible architecture. Content carries structure. |
